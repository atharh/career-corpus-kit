## Scope by level

Product ladders level on **how ambiguous the problem is and how much guidance was needed** as
much as on scope — that axis carries more weight here than in most crafts, so it leads.

| | What is owned | Ambiguity and guidance | What success is made of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Associate PM** | part of a team's roadmap, alongside a PM | works inside a defined strategy; needs support to scope and to decide | the problem was stated clearly and the thing shipped |
| **Product manager** | the team's roadmap and its rationale | creates clarity in *unambiguous* areas, with guidance | a shipped bet with a measured outcome, and a rationale a stranger can follow |
| **Senior PM** | a product area or use case, its strategy and its outcomes | creates clarity in *ambiguous* areas, with limited guidance | aggregate impact across several bets, and other teams' plans bend to the strategy |
| **Principal PM** | the most ambiguous and impactful strategies across teams; decomposes them into remits others own | creates clarity in *very ambiguous* areas, with no guidance | a strategy other teams execute without the author, and a market position that holds |

## Dimensions

- **The problem statement, written and then revised.** Not the launch summary — the artifact
  the team worked from, and how it changed as they learned. A story where the problem was
  correct from the first draft is a story where nobody learned anything.
- **First-hand customer contact, counted and specific.** How many, from which segments, and —
  the load-bearing part — a belief the author held that customer contact killed. Three friendly
  calls that confirmed the plan is not evidence.
- **Research they defined rather than consumed.** The question they went looking for, not the
  deck someone handed them. Senior levels are graded on framing the study, not on reading it.
- **Metrics defined before the launch**, including the number that would have said *stop*.
  Metrics chosen afterwards select themselves, and everyone in the room knows it.
- **The outcome, measured, with a counterfactual.** What moved, over what baseline, and what
  else was happening at the same time. A launch that did not move the number, honestly
  reported, is worth more here than three that supposedly did.
- **Competitive or market insight that changed a decision** — not a landscape summary. Which
  competitor behaviour or industry shift altered what got built, and what would have been built
  otherwise.
- **The prioritisation argument, with the rejected alternatives named.** Why these and not
  those, in a form a reader outside the team can follow. A roadmap without its discarded
  options is a list, not a strategy.
- **Scoping down, and what it cost.** What got cut to ship sooner, who objected, and whether
  the smaller thing actually taught them what the bigger one would have.
- **The riskiest assumption, exposed before the build.** Named up front, with the cheapest test
  that could have falsified it, and what the test returned.
- **Shipped to learn.** The beta or experiment, the close-out written down, and the decision
  that changed because of it — including a time the result killed the author's own idea.
- **The whole customer experience, not just the feature.** Pricing and packaging, launch
  narrative, sales and support enablement, onboarding for new and existing customers, docs.
  This is the most reliable separator between a PM who ships features and one who ships
  products.
- **A decision made fast on incomplete information**, with what was unknown at the time and how
  it turned out. Product ladders grade decisiveness explicitly; hindsight-free telling is the
  whole test.
- **Alignment won from someone who disagreed.** Which stakeholder, what they wanted, and what
  actually changed on each side. Alignment nobody resisted is not alignment.
- **A strong opinion dropped on evidence**, publicly, early enough to matter.
- **Shipping cadence as a measured thing** — what the team's rhythm was before and after, and
  what the author did to it, as distinct from what got shipped.
- **Somebody who got better.** PMs have no reports, so mentorship goes unrecorded almost by
  default — which is exactly why naming it separates senior tellings from ordinary ones.
- **The claim boundary.** Which parts were engineering's, design's, research's and the PM's.
  The most credible product stories are precise here.

## Named artifacts

Problem statement or brief, product requirements doc, roadmap with its written rationale,
strategy doc, research plan and its findings, experiment or beta plan, close-out report,
success-metric definition agreed before launch, dashboard, pricing and packaging proposal,
launch and GTM plan, sales and support enablement material, onboarding flow, decision log,
post-launch review.

## How stories in this craft overclaim

**The team's output as the author's impact.** *"I shipped X"* when engineering built it,
design designed it and research validated it. The PM's real claim is narrower and stronger:
which problem was chosen over which alternatives, what got cut, what sequence, and which calls
were theirs.

**Post-hoc metric attribution.** A revenue or engagement number that moved during the launch
window, presented as the launch's effect, with no holdout, no counterfactual and no mention of
the pricing change that shipped the same month. This is the single most common inflation in
product stories and the easiest for an interviewer to open up.

**The roadmap presented as strategy.** A list of what got built, described as a strategic
choice, with the rejected options missing — so there is no way to tell whether a choice was
made at all.

**Customer anecdote as research.** *"Customers told us"* standing on a handful of self-selected
conversations, usually with the customers most likely to agree.

## Pillar blind spots

**Talent** — no direct reports means growth work goes unrecorded, though senior product ladders
grade it. **Culture** — cross-functional influence gets told as outcomes rather than as the
relationships and the disagreements that produced them. And inside **results**, the missing
piece is almost always the *counterfactual*: product stories are rich in numbers and thin on
what would have happened anyway.
