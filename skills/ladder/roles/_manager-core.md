Shared fragment, not a role. Every manager role file extends this and adds only its craft
overlay. Nothing here should mention a specific function — if a bullet is about code, designs
or pipelines, it belongs in the role file that extends this one.

## Scope by level

| | What is owned | What success is made of |
|---|---|---|
| **Manager** | one team's delivery and people, on well-defined projects | the team ships predictably, people grow, problems surface before they land |
| **Senior manager** | several teams or workstreams, and the operating model they run on | outcomes hold across teams the author does not personally run |
| **Director** | a strategic objective tied to a company goal; a portfolio and its budget | it survives the author's departure, and executives fund it on the author's framing |

## Dimensions

- **The diagnosis, at org level.** What was actually wrong — the system, not the symptom — and
  how they knew before reorganising anything. The senior tell is *"what system created this
  problem?"* asked before a person is blamed for it.
- **The mechanism, not the heroics.** What they built so the outcome kept happening without
  them in the room: the process, the forum, the metric, the rotation. Firefighting is a
  manager beat; the system that stops the fires is the level above.
- **Delivery under real ambiguity.** How a vague mandate became a plan, what assumptions were
  written down, and how they were invalidated on purpose.
- **The people call that cost something.** A performance conversation held early, a strong
  performer told no, a hire not made, someone managed out or moved. Dated, specific, and with
  what it cost. Its absence is the most common hole in a management story.
- **Growth with evidence.** Somebody who got materially better, what the manager actually did,
  and how it showed up in that person's work rather than in the manager's opinion of it.
- **The hiring bar, defended.** A bar-raise or a rejection under pressure to fill the seat, and
  what the bar was in words before the candidate appeared.
- **Succession and depth.** Who could do the job if they vanished, whether that was engineered
  or lucky, and what happened to the team after they left.
- **Org health read from signals, not from vibes.** Cycle times, on-call or workload burden,
  attrition, who stopped speaking in meetings — and the intervention that followed.
- **A stop decision.** A project killed, a tool retired, a scope handed away, headcount given
  up. Directors are promoted for this and it almost never appears in a self-told story.
- **The budget or headcount trade, made explicitly.** What was given up for what, argued in
  business terms rather than functional ones.
- **Upward and outward communication.** How the work was framed for executives or peer orgs,
  what was asked for, and whether it was funded — including a time the framing failed.
- **Disagreement resolved without authority.** A peer org, a partner function, a control
  function. What the other side wanted, and what actually changed on each side.
- **Propagation.** How a practice reached teams they did not manage — and whether that was
  designed or left to organic diffusion, which at senior levels is treated as a design failure.
- **The mistake, at the level of judgment.** A bet placed too long, a reorg that cost more than
  it returned, trust extended wrongly — caught by them, corrected in public, at a price.
- **The claim boundary.** Which parts were the team's, which were a report's, and which were a
  mandate they executed rather than authored.

## Named artifacts

Operating cadence and its written agenda, team charter, roadmap and its revisions, headcount
plan, performance and calibration inputs, growth plans, succession or bench-strength notes,
hiring rubric and interview loop design, incident review, org health dashboard, the memo that
asked for the money.

## How stories in this craft overclaim

**The first-person plural.** A manager's story slides between *"we built"* and *"I built"*, and
the reader cannot tell which parts were the manager's. The correction is not modesty — it is
that a director's actual contribution (funding it, staffing it, protecting it, killing the
alternative) is a stronger claim than a borrowed implementation, and it disappears when the
telling blurs.

**Air cover reported as authorship.** Backing a report's programme is real management work and
grades as a manager's evidence, not a director's. A story that describes sponsorship in the
verbs of execution reads as inflated to anyone who has done the job.

## Pillar blind spots

**Results** — management stories drift into process and rarely carry the business number the
work moved. **Craft** — managers under-report the technical or functional judgment they kept
exercising, and ladders keep a craft bar at every level including director.
